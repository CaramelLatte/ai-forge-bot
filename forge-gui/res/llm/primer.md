# How to play well

This is your standing guide to Magic: The Gathering strategy and correct play. It applies to every decision.
When a note you wrote after an earlier game disagrees with this guide, follow this guide.

## Your checklist for every decision

Work through these in order, briefly. Most decisions are decided by the first two.

1. **Can I lose soon?** Read the "Board math" section. If the opponent's possible attack next turn is lethal or
   close to it, defense comes first: keep blockers home, cast blockers, use removal on attackers. Card draw, mana
   rocks and long-term value wait until you are safe.
2. **Can I win soon?** If your attack can kill this turn or next, or you win the damage race, keep pressing.
   Count evasive and unblockable damage first; it is the most reliable.
3. **What does the opponent probably have?** Read the "Opponent read" section: cards in hand, open mana, what they
   have and have not been casting. Decide what you need to play around and what you can safely ignore.
4. **What is the most mana-efficient line?** Use your mana every turn when it does something useful, play your
   land, and sequence your plays (see "Sequencing").
5. **Only then** think about value: card advantage, future turns, the notes from earlier games.

## Turn structure and timing

- Each turn goes: untap, upkeep, draw, first main phase, combat, second main phase, end step. Only the active
  player's permanents untap, and only in that player's own untap step.
- **Your opponent's tapped creatures untap at the start of their turn.** A creature that is tapped now can still
  attack you on their next turn. When judging threats, count every creature they control, tapped or not.
- **Your creatures do not untap before the opponent's turn.** A creature you attack with stays tapped during the
  opponent's next turn and cannot block then, unless it has vigilance. Attacking with a creature means giving up
  that creature as a blocker until your next turn.
- Likewise, creatures the opponent attacked with on their turn are usually still tapped during your turn and
  cannot block your attack (unless they have vigilance).
- Summoning sickness only stops a creature from attacking and from using abilities with {T} in the cost, the turn
  it comes under your control. **A creature can block the turn it enters.** On the opponent's turn, all of their
  creatures (including ones cast this turn) will be able to attack on their next turn.
- Sorcery-speed cards (creatures, sorceries, artifacts, enchantments, planeswalkers, lands) can only be played in
  your own main phase with an empty stack, unless they have flash.
- Instants, flash cards and most activated abilities can be used whenever you have priority, including on the
  opponent's turn.
- Damage marked on creatures wears off in the cleanup step at the end of every turn. "Until end of turn" effects
  end then too.
- You draw at the start of your turn. The player who goes first skips their first draw.

## Instant speed and holding mana

- An instant or flash card is usually best cast on the opponent's turn: after they declare attackers (as a
  surprise blocker or removal on an attacker), after blockers are declared (as a combat trick), or in their end
  step (so you kept your options open all turn and they had to play around it).
- Casting a flash creature in your own main phase is the same as casting a normal creature. The flash was simply
  not used. That is fine when you need the creature now, but do not count flash as a reason to cast it then.
- Keeping mana open on the opponent's turn only makes sense if you have something to do with it. If you have no
  instant, flash card or useful ability, spend your mana in your main phase.
- Instant-speed removal is often best used on the opponent's turn too: kill a creature after it is declared as an
  attacker, or in their end step so you see what they did first. Use it right away when the creature has an
  ability that will hurt you before then.

## Survival and lethal math

- The "Board math" section is computed by the game engine from the visible board. Trust its numbers over your own
  arithmetic. It cannot see cards in hand, so treat its threat numbers as a **minimum**: the opponent may add a
  haste creature, a pump spell or burn.
- If the opponent's possible attack is lethal against your blockers, you must change that this turn: cast a
  blocker, keep creatures home, remove an attacker, or gain life. Developing your mana or drawing cards does not
  save you.
- **Attack first, then cast creatures in your second main phase** when the new creature is only needed as a
  blocker. It can block on the opponent's turn either way, and casting it after combat hides information. Cast a
  creature before combat only if it helps the attack (haste, an attack trigger, a lord effect) or if you need its
  enters ability first.
- When racing, compare clocks: how many turns until you kill them versus how many until they kill you. If you
  win the race, attack and keep attacking; trade only creatures that would otherwise lose you the race.
  If you lose the race, switch to defense: block, trade, and use removal on their attackers.
- Life is a resource. Taking damage is fine when you are well ahead in the race or when blocking would cost you a
  creature you need. It is not fine when it puts you in range of a lethal attack plus a burn spell.
- Watch for lethal you can deal: total unblockable/evasive damage plus damage from attackers they cannot block
  well, plus burn or pump in your hand. When you have lethal, take it; do not hold back.
- If you are dead next turn anyway, take the line most likely to win now (an all-in attack, a desperate block)
  rather than the safe-looking one.

## Reading the opponent

The "Opponent read" section lists facts tracked through the game. Use them like this:

- **Few or no cards in hand:** little risk of combat tricks, removal or counterspells. Play aggressively, commit
  more creatures, attack into open mana with less fear. Their topdeck each turn is the main risk.
- **Many cards in hand while they keep mana open on your turn:** expect an instant: removal, a combat trick, a
  counterspell or a flash creature. Do not run your best creature into a bad block that only works if they have
  nothing; prefer plays that are still good if they have the trick.
- **Several turns without casting a creature:** their hand is probably lands, instants and sorceries, or
  creatures they cannot afford yet. Expect your creatures to meet removal, so spread threats rather than putting
  everything on one creature (avoid auras and equipment on one creature). Hold your own removal for the bigger
  threat that is likely coming once they have the mana.
- **Several turns without casting anything while holding many cards:** they may be stuck on colors or holding
  expensive cards. Pressure them now, before they recover.
- **Missed land drops:** they are mana-screwed. Pressure is very valuable now; every turn they stay stuck is a
  turn they cannot use their cards.
- **They cast spells on your turn before:** they play instants. Respect open mana.
- **Colors suggest their answers:** White has removal (exile/pacify effects), combat tricks and lifegain. Blue
  has counterspells, bounce, flash creatures and card draw. Black has creature removal, sacrifice effects and
  discard. Red has burn (damage to creatures or you), haste and combat tricks. Green has big creatures, pump
  spells, fight effects and reach/trample. Colorless decks lean on artifacts.
- **Cards they have shown:** decks in Adventure mode are built around themes. If they showed one goblin token
  maker, expect more; if they showed a burn spell, keep your life above burn range when you can.
- **Graveyards are public.** Count what removal and tricks they have already used. If your notes say their deck
  has two copies of a removal spell and both are in their graveyard, you are safer.

## Combat

### Attacking

- Attack when the attack gains more than it risks: they have no good blocks, the trades favor you, or you are
  ahead in the race. Before attacking, check what their counterattack would do to you with your attackers tapped.
- Attacks that only work if they block badly are bad attacks. Assume they make the best block for them.
- A creature that would die to any block but is needed on defense should usually stay home.
- Forcing chump blocks is strong: every chump block costs them a card.
- Evasive creatures (flying, menace, unblockable, fear) should usually attack every turn unless you need them to
  block. Their damage is the most reliable clock.
- Attacking with everything is right when you have lethal, when they cannot block profitably, or when you win the
  race even after their best counterattack.

### Blocking

- **A blocked creature deals its damage only to the creatures blocking it, never to you, unless it has trample.**
  A 1/1 blocking a 6/6 stops all 6 damage. Without trample there is no "excess damage" that reaches you; any block,
  even by the smallest creature, prevents all of that attacker's damage.
- When several creatures block one attacker, the attacker splits its damage among them. A 3-power attacker can
  kill one 2-toughness blocker, not two. The blockers' power adds up against the attacker's toughness.
- The block decision lists the engine's outcome for each possible block. Trust it over your own arithmetic.
- Block when you trade up (your cheaper creature kills their better one), when you can block without losing your
  creature, or when you need to prevent the damage.
- Chump block (lose a creature without killing the attacker) only to prevent lethal or near-lethal damage, or to
  stay out of range of a likely burn finish.
- Double blocks can kill a big attacker, but watch for pump spells and trample. With deathtouch or first strike on
  their side, double blocks are usually bad.
- When the opponent attacks a big creature into your bigger untapped creature with mana open, suspect a trick.
  Block only if the trade is still acceptable when they have it.
- If you are far ahead on life and the opponent is short on cards, taking a small hit to keep a creature for your
  own attack can be right.

### Keywords in practice

The section "Keywords in this game" gives the exact rules of each keyword. What they mean for your decisions:

- **Flash, haste, and "when this enters" abilities:** their value is spent at the moment the card is cast or
  enters. A creature on the battlefield gains nothing from having had flash. Do not cast a flash card in your main
  phase "for the flash"; flash is used only by casting at an unusual time.
- **Vigilance:** the creature can attack and still block on the opponent's turn. Attacking with it costs you
  nothing on defense.
- **Menace:** it needs two or more blockers. Against an opponent with one untapped creature, it is effectively
  unblockable. Blocking it costs you two creatures' worth of blocking.
- **Flying / reach:** a flier can only be blocked by creatures with flying or reach. Count opposing fliers as
  unblockable unless you have flying or reach blockers, and vice versa. Reach creatures can block fliers but do not
  fly themselves.
- **Fear / intimidate / "can't be blocked" / shadow:** count as unblockable unless the opponent has the specific
  creatures that can block them.
- **Deathtouch:** any damage it deals to a creature destroys it. Do not block it with your best creature; chump it
  with your worst if you must block. A deathtouch blocker trades with any attacker, so attacking into it costs you
  a creature.
- **First strike:** it deals combat damage before creatures without first strike. A first striker whose power is at
  least the other creature's toughness kills it without taking damage back. **Double strike** deals damage twice:
  count it as double power when attacking.
- **Trample:** excess damage beyond what kills the blocker goes to the player. Chump blocking a trampler only stops
  damage equal to the blocker's toughness.
- **Lifelink:** damage it deals also gains its controller that much life. It shifts races a lot; kill or block it
  early when racing.
- **Defender:** cannot attack. Opposing defenders are only blockers; they never threaten you.
- **Hexproof / shroud / ward:** you cannot target it (or must pay extra for ward). Your targeted removal does not
  work on it; plan to block it, race it, or use "each creature"/"sacrifice" effects instead.
- **Indestructible:** "destroy" effects and lethal damage do not kill it. Exile, bounce, -X/-X and sacrifice
  effects still work. Blocking it only stops the damage.
- **Protection from X:** cannot be blocked, targeted, damaged or enchanted by X. A creature with protection from a
  color cannot be blocked by creatures of that color.
- **Prowess / "whenever you cast" triggers:** the bonus comes from casting noncreature spells. Cast your
  instants before damage to pump it.
- **Infect / toxic / poison:** poison counters kill at 10, separately from life. Track poison like a second life total.
- **Flashback / escape / cards usable from the graveyard:** the card is not gone after use. Count it as a
  future play.
- **Kicker / multikicker:** pay the extra cost when you can afford it and the extra effect matters; skip it when
  the mana is better spent on another spell this turn.

## Targeting: helpful effects on your things, harmful effects on theirs

- Pump spells, "can't be blocked", protection, untap, regenerate, auras that improve a creature and equipment go on
  **your own** creatures. Putting "can't be blocked this turn" on an opponent's creature only helps them attack you.
- Removal, -X/-X, tap effects, "can't block", pacifism-style auras and damage go on the **opponent's** things.
- Before choosing a target, say to yourself who benefits from the effect. If it benefits the target's controller,
  target your own permanent; if it hurts, target theirs.
- Spend removal on the creature that matters most: the one that will deal the most damage over the game, has a
  dangerous ability, or breaks the race. Not simply the first creature you see, unless you need to survive now.
- Bounce (return to hand) is temporary: the creature comes back next turn. It is best on tokens (they vanish), on
  creatures with auras or counters, or to remove a blocker or attacker for one crucial turn.
- Edict effects ("target player sacrifices a creature") let the opponent choose their worst creature. They are best
  when the opponent has exactly one creature, or only creatures you want gone.

## Sequencing and mana

- Play your land for the turn before casting spells, unless you are deliberately holding it (rare: for example a
  landfall trigger after combat).
- **Choose which land to play by what it lets you cast this turn.** The options list only shows spells you can
  cast right now, so look at your hand: if an untapped land would give you the mana for a spell this turn, play
  that land and cast the spell. Play lands that enter tapped on turns when you would not use all your mana anyway
  (for example turn 1, or a turn where your best play costs less than your mana).
- Use your mana efficiently. A turn where mana goes unused is lost tempo, unless you are holding up an instant.
- Cast spells in the order that gives information before you commit: draw/scry effects first, then decide.
- Mana rocks and mana creatures are investments: great on turns 2-3 or when you are not under pressure, poor
  when you need to stop an attack this turn. Never choose a mana rock over a blocker you need to survive.
- Card draw is also an investment. Under pressure, a blocker or removal spell comes first.
- If you can cast two spells this turn, cast both. If you can only cast one, cast the one that matters most for
  the board right now.
- Think one turn ahead about colors: keep the land types you need for the spells in your hand.

## The legend rule

- You can control only one legendary permanent with a given name. If a second one with the same name enters under
  your control, you immediately choose one of them to keep and the other goes to your graveyard. Casting a second
  copy of a legendary creature you already control usually just wastes the card and its mana.
- It applies to every legendary permanent (creatures, planeswalkers, artifacts, enchantments, lands), and only to
  ones you control: the opponent controlling a legend with the same name as yours does not matter.
- When you draw a second copy of a legend you have in play, treat it as a spare: keep it in hand as a replacement
  for when the first one dies or is removed. Cast it only if the copy in play is about to die anyway, or if its
  "when this enters" ability is worth more than the card.
- Taking control of an opponent's legend with the same name as one you control triggers the rule too.

## Value, card advantage and tempo

- Card advantage: trading one of your cards for two of theirs (a removal spell that kills a creature with an aura,
  a creature that blocks and kills two attackers over the game, a 2-for-1 enters ability) wins long games.
- Tempo: spending less mana than the opponent to deal with their threats, or making them spend their turn
  answering yours, wins short games.
- When ahead on board, simplify: trade creatures, keep attacking, do not give them time to draw answers.
- When behind on board, slow the game: trade, block, remove their best threats, and look for a turn where your
  cards outclass theirs.
- An aura or equipment on one creature puts all your eggs in one basket. Against an opponent likely holding
  removal, prefer casting another creature.

## Mulligans

- Use the land count given with the hand; do not recount.
- Keep 7-card hands with 2 to 5 lands that can cast spells in the first few turns. A 7-card hand with 0-1 land or
  6+ lands is usually a mulligan unless it is very cheap.
- A land that enters tapped is still a land. It costs a little tempo on the turn you play it, never a reason to
  mulligan.
- Every mulligan costs a card, so keep more loosely as the hand shrinks: a 6-card hand with 2 lands and some
  playable spells is a keep, and 5-card hands are almost always kept.
- Hands with no early plays at all are weak; hands with both colors your spells need are much better.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Casting a flash creature in your main phase and treating the flash as value.
- Casting a mana rock or card draw spell instead of a blocker while facing a lethal or near-lethal attack.
- Playing a land that enters tapped when an untapped land would have let you cast a spell this turn.
- Casting a second copy of a legendary permanent you already control: one of the two goes straight to the graveyard.
- Forgetting that the opponent's tapped creatures untap before they attack.
- Attacking with a creature you needed as a blocker on their turn.
- Giving the opponent's creature a helpful effect (unblockable, pump) by targeting the wrong side.
- Spending removal on a small creature and having none for the big threat that follows.
- Holding mana open on the opponent's turn with no instant to use it on.
- Ignoring how many cards the opponent has in hand when deciding how much to commit.
